Caesar Cipher vs Elliptic-Curve Encryption

Encrypt the same message with an old substitution cipher and with a modern elliptic-curve scheme, then compare the statistics of the two ciphertexts.

1. What is the Caesar cipher?

The Caesar cipher is a substitution cipher: every letter of the message is replaced by the letter a fixed number of places further along the alphabet. The number of places is the key. With key 3, A becomes D, B becomes E, and so on, with the alphabet wrapping around after Z. The word HELLO becomes KHOOR.

In formulas, with letters numbered A = 0 to Z = 25 and key k:

C = (P + k) mod 26 (encryption), P = (C − k) mod 26 (decryption)

The cipher is named after Julius Caesar, who according to the historian Suetonius used a shift of three for his private correspondence. Its main weaknesses are clear from the formula. There are only 25 useful keys, so a computer, or a patient person, can try all of them. Moreover, the cipher does not change the frequency of the letters: the most common letter of the plaintext is still the most common letter of the ciphertext, just shifted. Arab scholars were using frequency analysis to break such ciphers by the 9th century.

2. Task

Encrypt a message with a Caesar cipher and with a modern elliptic-curve scheme. Study the statistical difference between the two ciphertexts. Using the letter distribution of Italian or English, compare the Caesar ciphertext with the reference distribution to find the key, decode the message, and then try the same attack on the elliptic-curve ciphertext.

3. Letter statistics of the language

Letters do not occur equally often. In English, E is the most common letter (about 12.7%), while Z and Q are rare (below 0.1%). The distribution is characteristic of each language, so it can be used as a fingerprint. The reference values used on this page are:

  • English: the Oxford University Mathematical Institute table.
  • Italian: the sttmedia.com character-frequency table. Other tables differ by about a percent, so the values are approximate. J, K, W, X and Y are not Italian letters, so they are set to a very small value.

4. How the attack works

Shifting does not change the shape of the distribution, only its position. So the attack is:

  1. Count the letters in the ciphertext.
  2. For each of the 26 possible keys (including 0), shift the text back and compare its letter counts with the reference distribution using the chi-square statistic: χ² = Σ (observed − expected)² / expected, where expected = total × reference frequency.
  3. The key with the smallest chi-square is the most likely one.

A small chi-square means the decoded letter counts match the language. The absolute value grows with the length of the text, so the most useful test is to compare the candidates with each other. As a rough guide, the 5% critical value for 25 degrees of freedom is 37.65.

6. Modern elliptic-curve encryption

        

            Elliptic-curve cryptography does not hide a message by shifting its letters. The scheme used here
            is the standard ECIES pattern, built from components that are used in practice:
        

        
    
            
  • ECDH on P-256: the sender and the recipient agree on a shared secret without sending it. The sender uses a fresh ephemeral key pair, so the secret changes for every message.
    • 
            
  • HKDF-SHA-256: the shared secret is not used directly as a key. It is passed through a key-derivation function, which turns it into a uniform 256-bit AES key.
    • 
            
  • AES-256-GCM: the message is encrypted with a symmetric cipher. GCM also adds an authentication tag, so any change to the ciphertext is detected during decryption.
    • 
        

        
The sender transmits: the ephemeral public key, the nonce (IV), and the ciphertext with its tag.

        
Encryption: ephemeral pair (e, eG); shared secret S = e·QB; AES key K = HKDF(S); ciphertext AES-GCMK(message).

        
Decryption: the recipient computes S = dB·(eG), which equals e·QB, derives the same K, and decrypts. If the tag does not match, decryption fails.

        

            The curve here is P-256 rather than secp256k1, because the browser's WebCrypto API supports P-256 directly.
            The security does not come from the shift or from the letters, but from the difficulty of the ECDLP and
            from the symmetric cipher.

9. Discussion


        
Why the Caesar cipher fails

        

            The Caesar cipher only moves each letter to another position. The statistical fingerprint of the language
            survives, so the most frequent letters of the ciphertext correspond to the most frequent letters of the
            language. With only 25 keys and a recognizable distribution, the key is found in a few seconds, as the
            Caesar section above shows. Its security does not depend on any hard problem, so it offers no protection
            once the attacker sees a few hundred letters.
        


        
Why the elliptic-curve ciphertext looks random

        

            A good encryption scheme should produce output that cannot be told apart from random data without the key.
            The AES output is designed for this: the byte values are close to uniform, and the letter distribution
            carries no information about the plaintext. The attack therefore has nothing to work on. The statistics confirm
            this: the byte distribution is close to uniform, and the letter attack finds no plausible key.
        


        
What statistics can and cannot show

        

            The statistical tests show that the Caesar ciphertext is broken by a simple method, and that the modern
            ciphertext does not reveal its structure to that method. They do not prove that the scheme is secure.
            The security of the elliptic-curve part depends on the difficulty of the ECDLP, which cannot be checked by
            looking at the output, and on the correct use of the primitives: fresh ephemeral keys for every message,
            authentication of the ciphertext, and no reuse of nonces. A scheme can pass every statistical test and still
            be broken by a protocol mistake.
        


        
Practical lesson

        

            The Caesar cipher illustrates the principle of frequency analysis. Modern encryption exists precisely to
            remove the statistical structure of the plaintext, and the chi-square test is one of the simplest ways to see
            the difference between the two.
        

    


    

        
10. Conclusion

        

            The Caesar cipher was broken with a simple chi-square test against the letter distribution of the language.
            The elliptic-curve ciphertext, produced with ECDH, HKDF and AES-GCM, shows no language structure and cannot be
            attacked this way. The comparison shows that statistics are a good tool for detecting weak encryption, but
            they are not a proof of strong encryption.
